2. Differences between expected and actual workflows

  • Order and timing of approvals. P6 assumed all three tickets would run before any approval, so Accounting on 102 would see the $840 request pending. In practice MM approved #1 between runs, so 102 saw it paid. Every approval was made before the next ticket ran.
  • An extra run on 101. Not planned. After payment, 101 was run again. The Boss and Customer Service spent 22,532 tokens to confirm nothing had changed except the status.
  • Tickets went past approval. P6 expected each to stop at awaiting_human_approval. Because approvals happened, 101 reached waiting_on_vendor, 102 resolved and 103 in_progress.
  • The cash flag on 103 was missing. The plan expected Accounting to point out that $160 can't fund a restock of at least $264. Accounting's cash check returned $160 with no obligations, and its report didn't mention the shortfall.
  • Request numbers. As predicted: #1 (101), #2 (102), #3 (103).
  • Delegation count. P6 expected 7 hand-offs. The actual total was 8 across four runs: 2 + 1 (the re-run) + 2 + 3.
✍ My assessment

The missing cash flag on 103 matters most to me. It's what would have let me approve knowing the limitations. When I approved the $52.00 price, checking was already down to $160.00 after the two payments, and filling the order needs about 12 × $22.00 = $264.00 of restock. Nothing in Accounting's report or the approval card told me the shop couldn't afford that. So I was approving a discount on an order the shop can't currently fill.

It was a prompt problem, not a tool problem. The data was there: Accounting's get_cash_position call returned $160.00 with no other obligations, and evaluate_bulk_discount_request returned the restock cost (shortfall_restock_cost_at_unit_cost). The prompt told Accounting to check cash for payment requests, but not to compare remaining cash against the cost of fulfilling a discounted order. A line such as "before filing a price override, compare cash after obligations with the restock cost, and say plainly if the shop can't fund it" would likely fix it. The other differences, such as approvals happening between runs and the extra 101 run, came from how I used the dashboard rather than from the agents.

  • Routing by the Boss. All four assignments followed the fixed rule in the Boss prompt (customer_order → Customer Service, rent_notice → Facilities, price_override → Accounting). Evidence that the split helped (evidence against a single agent)

  • Safeguards come from tool allowlists. Facilities and Customer Service can't file payments, and no agent can approve one. Every P9 payment request came from Accounting, and no agent called a human-only tool. With one agent, those guarantees would rest on the MCP-side checks alone.
  • Inventory's vendor choice. In the P5 review (harness.md §12), Accounting alone listed the courier and the mug vendor as hoodie restock sources. With the hand-off to Inventory, the P9 run picked the right vendor and explained why ("the other listed vendors do not fit this product").
  • Customer Service drafts. Customer Service produced customer-facing drafts that stuck to the facts. On 103 it kept the unapproved price out of the promise, as its prompt requires.
  • The audit trail shows who did what. Every action is attributable to a role (requested_by = accounting on all three requests).

What further evidence would support the single-agent argument

  • Run the same three tickets with one agent given the union of the specialists' tools (still without human-only tools), and compare: tokens, time, request amounts, whether it files a payment only after verifying (102), whether it picks Bulldog Print Co (103), and whether it escalates the unpaid invoice (101).

  • Compare outcomes against the same checks used in section 1, not just token cost.
✍ My assessment

I think the multi-agent setup was unnecessary for every step. In practice the person using this dashboard is the boss. They read the ticket and already know who should handle it, so having a Boss agent decide routing is a boss delegating to another boss so it can delegate: delegating the delegation. That's overkill.

The evidence supports this:

  • All four of the Boss agent's assignments followed the fixed type-to-agent rule in its prompt, yet the Boss used 53,704 of 138,370 tokens (39%), and 72% of the 101 re-run.
  • Hand-offs often just passed facts along. Facilities verified the lease and handed everything to Accounting. Inventory re-ran the same tool Accounting had already called.
  • Six reads were repeated by a second agent.

A simpler design: let the human pick the specialist (or a single agent with the right tools) directly from the dashboard, and keep the tool restrictions and human approvals, which do the real safety work.

What I'd keep from the current design: the role-based tool limits meant only Accounting could file payment requests, and in P5 a separate Inventory step caught a wrong vendor choice. The safeguards don't depend on the Boss agent, so removing it shouldn't weaken them.

6. Your overall reflection

✍ My assessment

Overall, it was surprisingly good. On all three tickets the agents routed exactly as I planned in P6, used the right MCP tools, found the real blockers, and never moved money without my approval. Every payment was approved by MM, and cash reconciled to $160.00. Inventory picked the right vendor, and the customer drafts were accurate and careful not to promise unapproved prices.

Where it fell short:

  • Prompting: the missing cash flag on 103.
  • Design: a Boss agent that duplicates what the human user already knows.
  • Action: no way to act on the agents' own recommendations.

I think with better prompting, especially telling Accounting to weigh cash against the cost of fulfilling an order, it could be better. A simpler structure with the human as the router, plus recommended next steps in the dashboard, would make it more useful as a real tool for running the shop.
